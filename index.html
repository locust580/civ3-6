Intellectual Roots
The ideas behind this game

Vannevar Bush (1945)

In "As We May Think," Bush envisioned the memex: a desk-sized machine storing all of a person's books and records, retrievable by associative trails — a precursor to hypertext. He saw technology as a way to extend human memory and thought, not replace them. Crucially, his vision was wrong in its specifics (microfilm desks) yet prophetic in its structure (the web, search engines).

Douglas Engelbart (1962–1968)

Engelbart's "Augmenting Human Intellect" argued that computers should amplify human capability, not automate humans away. His NLS system (demonstrated in the "Mother of All Demos") introduced the mouse, video conferencing, hypertext, and collaborative editing — decades early. For Engelbart, the goal was always a symbiosis: human + machine smarter than either alone.

Erik Brynjolfsson — The Turing Trap

Brynjolfsson warns that framing AI as "achieving human-level performance" is a trap. It encourages automation of human tasks rather than augmentation. The economic gains from AI replacing workers concentrate in capital; gains from AI augmenting workers spread more widely. The choice of framing has enormous consequences for who benefits.

The Third Path

Beyond augmentation vs. automation lies a less-discussed possibility: genuinely new forms of intelligence — hybrid biological-digital minds, collective intelligence networks, post-symbolic cognition — that don't map onto either human intelligence or current AI. Predicting these is like Bush predicting TikTok from microfilm.
